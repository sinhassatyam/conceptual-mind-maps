# FLP Impossibility in Distributed Systems

> Based on: Fischer–Lynch–Paterson’s original paper “Impossibility of Distributed Consensus with One Faulty Process” and Henry Robinson’s “A Brief Tour of FLP Impossibility”.

---

## 1. What FLP Actually Says

The **FLP impossibility result** (Fischer, Lynch, Paterson, 1985) states:

> In a fully asynchronous distributed system with reliable message delivery and the possibility of even a single crash failure, there is **no deterministic consensus algorithm** that is guaranteed to terminate in all admissible executions.

Consensus is defined by three properties:

- **Termination**  
  Every non-faulty (correct) process eventually decides on a value.
- **Agreement**  
  No two correct processes decide on different values.
- **Validity**  
  The value decided must have been proposed by at least one process.

FLP proves: for any deterministic consensus algorithm in this model, there exists at least one admissible execution in which **termination never happens**, even though the algorithm is correct in all finite prefixes of that execution.

---

## 2. System Model and Assumptions

FLP works in a specific, deliberately pessimistic model:

- **Asynchronous timing**
  - No bound on message delivery delay.
  - No bound on how long a process step may take.
  - Processes cannot distinguish a crashed process from a very slow one.

- **Crash failures**
  - At most **one process** may crash.
  - Once crashed, the process takes no more steps.
  - Crashed processes are exempt from deciding.

- **Reliable channels**
  - Channels do **not drop or corrupt messages**.
  - Every message sent is eventually delivered, unless the receiver has crashed.

These assumptions make the result very strong: even with at most one crash and no message loss, we cannot guarantee deterministic consensus termination.

---

## 3. Key Ideas: Configurations, Events, and Valency

The original proof uses the notion of **configurations** and **valency**.

### 3.1 Configurations and Events

- A **configuration** is the entire system state:
  - Local state of each process (registers, program counters, etc.).
  - Contents of the message buffer (messages in transit).

- An **event** is the delivery of a single message to a single process (or a local step).

A **run** (or schedule) is a sequence of events that moves the system from configuration to configuration.

### 3.2 Valency

Each configuration is associated with a **valency**:

- **0-valent**  
  From this configuration, all admissible runs lead to a decision of 0.
- **1-valent**  
  From this configuration, all admissible runs lead to a decision of 1.
- **Bivalent**  
  From this configuration, some admissible runs lead to a decision of 0 and others to a decision of 1.

Intuitively:

- 0-valent/1-valent configurations are “committed” to a particular decision.
- Bivalent configurations are “undecided”; the system can still go either way depending on future scheduling.

---

## 4. Proof Sketch

The proof has two key steps.

### 4.1 Step 1: Existence of a Bivalent Initial Configuration

Assume, for contradiction, that all initial configurations are univalent (0-valent or 1-valent).

Consider initial vectors of proposals (each process proposes 0 or 1). If we move from “all processes propose 0” to “all propose 1” by flipping one process at a time, there must be two adjacent initial configurations differing in only one process’s proposal where the valency changes from 0 to 1.

Since they differ in only one process’s local state, an indistinguishability argument shows that from such a configuration the system could evolve to either decision. That implies this configuration is **bivalent**, contradicting the assumption that all are univalent.

Therefore, **at least one initial configuration is bivalent**.

### 4.2 Step 2: Bivalence Can Be Preserved

Starting from a bivalent configuration, FLP shows:

- For any pending event `e`, there exists some finite run that **delays** `e` and applies other events first, leading to another **bivalent** configuration.

Intuition:

- If delivering `e` immediately always leads to a univalent configuration, then consider a different scheduling that delivers some other events before `e`.
- By carefully reasoning about the independence of events and indistinguishability of configurations, one can construct a path that keeps the system bivalent even after eventually delivering `e`.

This means a scheduler can always choose a sequence of events that preserves bivalence — i.e., keeps the system undecided.

### 4.3 Putting it Together

Starting from a bivalent initial configuration:

- Apply Step 2 repeatedly to construct an infinite admissible run (all messages eventually delivered, at most one crash) in which **no decision is ever reached**.
- That contradicts the termination property.

Hence, no deterministic consensus algorithm can guarantee termination in every admissible execution under FLP’s model.

---

## 5. Practical Meaning

FLP does **not** say consensus always fails; it says:

- There exists at least one adversarial run (schedule) where a deterministic consensus algorithm never terminates.
- In “normal” executions, consensus algorithms may terminate quickly.

In practice, systems work around FLP by **modifying assumptions**:

- **Partial synchrony**  
  Assume the system is asynchronous for some time but eventually becomes synchronous (bounded delays). Many Paxos/Raft correctness proofs use such models.
- **Randomization**  
  Use randomized algorithms: FLP’s impossibility is for deterministic algorithms; randomized consensus can terminate with probability 1.
- **Failure detectors**  
  Introduce oracles that provide (possibly imperfect) information about failures; with suitable failure detectors, consensus becomes solvable in asynchronous environments.

---

## 6. Where FLP Matters in System Design

Situations where FLP is directly relevant:

- **Leader election and membership**  
  Which node is the primary? Who is in the cluster?
- **Metadata/configuration services**  
  Systems like ZooKeeper/etcd/Consul rely on consensus to maintain a consistent log.
- **Distributed locks and coordination**  
  Ensuring only one actor holds a lock or performs a critical action.

Design implications:

- You cannot guarantee both **safety** (no conflicting decisions) and **liveness** (always making progress) under all timing and failure patterns in a fully asynchronous model.
- Real systems often choose to **preserve safety** and accept that, under extreme conditions, **progress may temporarily stall**.
